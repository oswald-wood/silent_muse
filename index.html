Prelude

On the Metaphysics of Watching

✦

There is a particular species of longing that no language has ever been generous enough to name — the kind that does not ache for what has been lost, but for what has never been possessed, for what perhaps exists only in the cathedral of one's own imagination. Philosophy has long circled the question of whether beauty is a property of the object or a covenant between the observer and the observed. I can tell you, with the quiet certainty of a man who has spent months studying a single, unknowing subject, that it is neither. Beauty, in its most radical and terrifying form, is a sudden surplus of meaning — an ambush. It is the moment when an ordinary Tuesday afternoon in an ordinary corridor cracks open like an overripe fruit, and out pours an entire universe you never suspected was hiding there, pressing against the thin membrane of the mundane.

You are a Geology student. You are supposed to be deciphering the language of the earth — reading its strata, its faults, its ancient, unhurried histories. But I wonder sometimes if the universe did not assign you that discipline as a cosmic joke, a wink in my direction. Because you yourself are geological in your constitution: layered, slow, carrying within you sediments of feeling that the undiscerning eye could never detect. To study you, as I have, requires the same patience, the same reverence, the same silent and sustained attention that a geologist brings to a rock face — the understanding that what is most profound is almost always invisible on the surface, and that revelation demands stillness.

We share no common ground. There are no overlapping social circles to bridge the chasm between your world and mine, no serendipitous introductions waiting to happen at some future crossroads. We are parallel lines drawn by a careless, cosmic architect — permitted to observe each other's trajectory, but forbidden by the unyielding geometry of fate to ever merge. The Greeks had a word, sonder, though they did not coin it: the realization that each passerby carries within them a life as vivid and complex and fully furnished as one's own. I have experienced sonder precisely once. It was in August, near the lift area of the Nalanda complex, when your eyes met mine for the length of a single, stolen heartbeat. And in that heartbeat, the full weight of your interior world pressed against me like a warm hand pressed flat against glass, and I have not recovered from it since.

There are unavoidable, immovable reasons — constructed by circumstance, by duty, by the particular configuration of stars that presided over our respective births — that make the probability of any formal union between us absolute zero. I know this. I have known it the way one knows the theorems of mathematics: not with regret, but with a kind of resigned, philosophical grace. The rational mind would prescribe action. It would urge courage — the manufactured introduction, the calculated vulnerability, the gamble. But they do not understand, those rational minds, the profound and sovereign completeness of the silent observer. They do not understand that there is a form of devotion so pure it does not require reciprocation to survive, a form of attention so whole it carries its own nourishment within itself, the way a camel carries water through deserts that never promised to end.

What follows is not a diary. Diaries are domestic things — vessels for the mundane, the grievance, the grocery list of the soul. What you hold is a sacred ledger. A curator's archive. A testament written by a man who has made a private religion out of attention — who has learned that to truly see another human being, without agenda, without demand, without the corrosive hunger of expectation, is perhaps the closest a mortal creature can come to an act of grace. Every entry here is a sighting, a meditation. Every line is the pencil-mark of an archaeologist mapping a civilization that does not know it is being mapped.

She was, each time, nothing less than a breath of fresh air. Not metaphorically — or not merely metaphorically. She was the sensation of cold water finding a dry mouth; the specific quality of morning light after a week of overcast skies; the first note of a song you have been trying to remember for years. Every encounter reset something in my chest that I had not known was wound too tight. Every sighting was a small, private resurrection.

Chapter the First

The Winter Panopticon

January 13th & 14th

The Green Solitude

The new year had arrived wearing the Kharagpur winter like a funeral shroud — grey and persistent and carrying within its cold the faint smell of coal smoke and damp earth. The campus had been emptied of its usual frenzy; even the birds seemed to have quietly resigned from their usual commentary. It was in this monochrome, muffled world that you made your first appearance of the season, and the effect was so precisely contrary to everything around you that I genuinely lost my footing for a moment, as though the ground itself had paused to take note.

You emerged near the MT hall wearing a green hoodie — and I want to be careful here, because the word "green" is laughably inadequate. This was not merely a colour. It was a declaration. A thesis statement. Against the unbroken pewter of the January sky, against the washed-out asphalt and the bleached architecture of the campus winter, you were the singular, defiant argument that colour had not abandoned the world — that somewhere, in some quiet corner of creation, warmth persisted. The hoodie was thick and enveloping, pulled slightly forward around your face, as though offering you a small, portable shelter from the world's sharp edges. You paired it with simple blue jeans that disappeared into the mist at the hem, and you walked — oh, the way you walked.

There is a philosophy of gait that has never, to my knowledge, been properly written. The way a person carries their body through space is their most honest autobiography — more truthful than any curriculum vitae, any social media presence, any carefully curated self-presentation. Your walk along the desolate stretch of road connecting to the Pepsi-cut was not a performance. It was entirely, magnificently self-possessed — each footfall placed with the unhurried certainty of someone who has made peace with the particular patch of earth they occupy. The world around you seemed to instinctively lower its volume. You moved through space the way a tuning fork moves through air: not loudly, but with a frequency that reorganises everything in proximity.

You were alone, and in your aloneness there was nothing of loneliness. This distinction matters enormously. What surrounded you was not loneliness — it was solitude: the curated, inhabited, fully-furnished interior of a person who has made genuine peace with her own company. I watched you from fifty metres away and felt the way one feels in a cathedral — small, and quiet, and not unwilling to remain so. The fact that me and Arnav were as usual doing our acts of shenanigans in front of your hall was a never-not-to-be-taken-granted phenomenon and I, despite all that, got enough sense of belonging to somehow find my sight probing towards the hall gate — seeing a tall, lean girl coming out briskly and moving straight towards Pepsi-cut. I remarked awkwardly that she was her, and Arnav wasn't sure this time. I, though, was. And I was already moving quicker than usual. We followed behind her, in steps which were full of tension but simultaneous excitement. I would for days mourn the presence of a thick-branched tree which separated us as you all of a sudden rotated 360 degrees, your point of turn masked by that shrewd tree. So I slowed my pace and could only see a little of your calm, composed face. The heartbeat was already ascending, as always.

I saw you again the next night and recognising you was a juxtaposition — not because of how far you were from me, but how difficult it was based on the haze, the dim lighting and the number of people you were with. You folded into the chaos near the roundabout — and this second sighting was its own revelation. In isolation, you had been a poem. In a crowd, you became a philosophical proposition. Because even surrounded by the full, cacophonous machinery of campus life, you remained entirely, inexplicably other. Not aloof. Not disdainful. Simply elsewhere, in some interior country whose geography was inaccessible to the noise pressing in from all sides. Almost 60 metres away, you with 4 of your friends, me with Arnav, and the first thing I notice far, far away was you. We changed routes, went behind you, first on the footpath which separated me and you, near the TSC. You disappeared twice while ahead of me — once maybe you went to VS hall, and then disappeared to Vegies, while we tried every permutation to go ahead of you and revert back to pass you. But everything failed. Finally, we saw you in Vegies. But I didn't have any strength left to look at you, so I didn't come. We saw you come out and go some other way, and time quickly went past 10:45. And then at 11, Arnav called me — reciting an incident worth its weight in gold that had bechanced near LLR-MMM road. He said: "She was smiling, laughing more like, her face breaking into one of the most delightful dreams — her with two of her friends, and they were in some discussion, the result of which was this big, big smile on her radiating, heavenly face." I went all the way to my room, some 600 steps from RP Hall gate, and of all the hundreds of things I could have thought of, all I saw was your smiling face.

January 20th  ·  9:57 AM

The Epiphany of Morning

The morning was one of those crystalline winter mornings that Kharagpur occasionally produces — the kind that feel less like weather and more like an argument. The air was sharp enough to taste, carrying in it the particular cold that settles in the lungs and makes one temporarily, pleasurably aware of the fact of breathing. I was standing near the agricultural gate footpath, half-attending to some thought I have long since forgotten, when you materialized at the edge of my peripheral vision and made every other thought irrelevant.

You wore a maroon sweatshirt that morning — a deep, wine-dark red that seemed to have been chosen with the deliberate intention of complementing the exact quality of January light, though of course you had chosen it for no such reason, which made it somehow more perfect. It fell over loose-fitting blue jeans with the unselfconscious ease of clothing that has been worn enough to conform to its wearer. The pale winter sun was brutal in its honesty that morning; it stripped away shadow, offered no flattering angles, made no accommodations. And in that unsparing, democratic light, a realization arrived in my chest with the force of a physical blow — not a gentle epiphany but a sudden, structural conviction, the kind that reorganises one permanently.

You are, without a solitary shred of hyperbole, the most beautiful human being I have ever seen. And I have spent a great many subsequent weeks interrogating that sentence, turning it in the light, checking it for the distortions of infatuation or projection. It has survived every such examination. It is not that your features are symmetrical in some classical, measurable sense — though they are — it is that your face is one of those rare human faces that appears to be organised around a principle rather than a template. As though the various components of it were not assembled but arrived at, the way a proof arrives at its conclusion: inevitably, and with a sense that no other arrangement could have been correct.

But yours was emphatically not a loud beauty. The world is full of beauty that announces itself, that demands to be seen, that wears its own visibility as armour. Yours was the opposite. It was quiet in the way that the deepest music is quiet — not silent, but operating below the threshold of noise, requiring of its perceiver a certain quality of stillness and receptivity. You completely lacked the performative extroversion that our contemporary world has decided is the compulsory costume of the young and attractive. There was no display. There was only a person walking through the morning air with the absolute conviction that she was sufficient as she was. And beneath the surface calm there lived something older and deeper — a luminescent melancholy. Not sadness in any clinical sense, but the particular gravity of a person who has felt things deeply and chosen to carry that depth privately, who smiles — and your smile is genuinely, dazzlingly beautiful — without ever quite allowing the smile to reach all the way to wherever the truest part of her lives. Keats wrote that beauty is truth, truth beauty. Standing near that gate in the January cold, I understood for the first time that he was not writing a metaphor. He was writing a field report. And of course, I knew what time I was supposed to get out of hall — all I had to do was depart my room at 9:45 every Tuesday to find you in the fleeting compass of Agri gate and Nalanda complex subway side before 10 AM. 9:54 AM was, I presume, this first time.

February 2nd  ·  12:02 PM

A Fleeting Anchor

The midday campus is a study in beautiful, overwhelming disorder — a ceaseless river of human movement in which every face is another anonymous story rushing toward its next obligation, its next meal, its next small crisis. I had been moving through this river in my customary fog when near NC143, right by the subway gate, you cut through the noise with the clean precision of a tuning fork's resonance. One moment there was only the undifferentiated crowd; the next, the crowd had rearranged itself around a fixed point, and that point was you.

It was, by any measurable standard, a trivial encounter. Fourteen seconds, perhaps. No eye contact. No proximity worth recording. You were simply there — moving with that unhurried, self-possessed certainty that is entirely your own — and then you were not. But in those fourteen seconds, something reset in my chest. The low-grade static of the day — all of it dropped away. You were, as you always are, a breath of fresh air: the specific sensation of stepping out of a stuffy room into an open sky, of suddenly remembering that the world is larger than its immediate pressures suggest. I was with my group of 3 — me, Ayush and Lakshay — and I was in a really bad mood because of reasons even I didn't know. I had rammed into a car side-view mirror while coming to the subway side, and perhaps Sawmbabu's PED-II test was one reason of anger. Coming back from the washroom, my bad mood evaporated in a wisp of smoke as I saw you jostling amidst a crowd going towards the lift area, far away in front of NC143. Kierkegaard wrote about the aesthetic stage of existence — the life lived in the pursuit of beauty, of the singular moments in which meaning erupts unbidden from the grey surface of experience. I wonder if he had ever found, in the mere fact of another person's continued, oblivious existence, a reason to believe in the benevolence of the day.

February 3rd

The Invisible Tether

Fate, on this afternoon, was in one of its crueller, more playful moods. It placed me, with no warning, directly behind you on the footpath. The distance between us was exactly one meter. One hundred centimetres. In the geometry of ordinary social life, this is nothing — a conversational distance. In the private geometry of what I was experiencing, it was the distance between a planet and its star: a span across which immense forces operate, and across which nothing physical can pass.

You were wearing the varsity jacket that day — the specific one from the induction team — and its familiarity was its own kind of violence, the intimacy of having learned to recognise your wardrobe with the unhurried scholarship of someone who has been paying attention for months. Light blue loose-fit jeans, and those skin-coloured Crocs. You walked ahead of me from NR122, all the way past the TP classes, and finally to the lift area, and through every step of this journey I was engaged in the most intense, the most silent, the most absolute act of attention I have ever performed.

I want to describe what it is like to walk one meter behind someone you admire without their knowledge. It is like being the only person in a concert hall who can hear the music. The world proceeds as normal around you but you are operating on a different frequency, receiving a signal so clear and so consuming that everything else becomes background noise. I was hyperaware of every footstep you placed — the specific sound your shoes made on the path, the rhythm of it, the way it altered slightly when you shifted your bag. I was aware of the faint displacement of air that one moving body creates ahead of another. I was aware, acutely and physically, that you were breathing — that you were engaged in the most ordinary of human acts — and I found in this awareness a tenderness so acute it almost made me stumble. Thanks to Arham I turned around to ask him where Arnav was — and a handshake too. I was in my best drip: a knitted blue sweater and black pants. I forgot entirely where I was for a second the moment I turned and saw you moving past me. Krishna could only smile a little. I couldn't control mine. We followed to the washroom as you and your friend went towards the lift area again. I was happy again.

February 9th  ·  11:58 AM to 12:04 PM

A Six-Minute Eternity

There is a philosophical thought experiment concerning the relativity of time — the observation that an hour spent in pleasant company feels like minutes, while an hour spent in agony stretches into geological epochs. I have a different, more specific datum to offer: six minutes spent in the vicinity of someone who fills your entire sensory world with a quality of light that ordinary life does not provide. Six minutes, and I can still narrate each one with the granular precision of someone who lived them at an entirely different speed than the clock suggested.

You wore a white and pink embroidered kurti that morning — delicate, worked in small patterns around the neckline and hem, the kind of craft that requires patience and an eye for what is worth beautifying. It caught the late February breeze in a way that made the fabric lift slightly, briefly, at the edges. Loose light blue jeans. Beige Crocs. And you were standing near the TP class stairs with your friend, deep inside a conversation that drew your entire attention, your head tilted slightly in that posture of genuine listening that I have come to recognise as distinctly yours.

The universe felt suddenly and dangerously small. There were perhaps forty feet between us and in the full, warm light of the February midday you were so entirely beautiful that I felt something close to vertigo. Not the vertigo of dizziness, but the vertigo that Rilke described in the Duino Elegies: the vertiginous awareness of standing at the edge of something immeasurably vast. Your friend noticed me — the subtle recalibration of her gaze, the slight narrowing that occurs when a person shifts from casual observation to focused assessment. Internal panic rose in my chest like floodwater. Arnav seized me by the neck — too suddenly, too urgently — and manufactured a performance of ordinary male banter, pulling me toward the washroom with an energy that had nothing to do with any genuine need, and releasing me abruptly once I was out of the immediate line of assessment. He parted, saying a goodbye I could only feel angry about — for there was no need for pulling me by the scruff of the neck. You perhaps noticed the shitshow we were doing. There was absolutely no need of that, but then male friendships don't work that easy way either. I returned to the shelter of a pillar. I stood there, rigid as carved marble, a sentry at the gate of a temple he was not permitted to enter. Krishna arrived, and together we watched, and the six minutes passed the way six minutes pass when every second of them contains the entire weight of a feeling you cannot name or spend or resolve — slowly, and not at all.

February 10th & 12th

The Duality of Light

You exist differently by day and by night, and both versions are complete.

On the morning of the 10th — 9:54 AM, the back gate of the agriculture department — you appeared in the familiar maroon KGP hoodie layered over a black t-shirt, blue jeans, Crocs. You were walking alongside your friend, your steps synchronised in that unconscious way that happens between people who know each other well enough to have stopped being careful. Your laughter — I caught the edge of it, carried toward me by a cooperative breeze — was unguarded. Spontaneous. It arrived in my chest without permission and stayed there for the rest of the day.

Two nights later — 10:20 PM, near the heritage signboard — the night had rearranged you entirely, and the result was devastating in an entirely different register. There is a reason that painters of the Baroque period were obsessed with chiaroscuro — because darkness does not diminish what it cannot touch; it concentrates it. In the dim, golden luminescence of the streetlamp, your features resolved into something beyond beauty into the territory of the archetypal: every portrait that Rembrandt ever pulled from darkness, every face that Vermeer ever tilted toward a window. You were a silhouette and a celestial body simultaneously. A quiet guardian watching over the sleeping campus. Arnav at his intuition level remarked there was too much stillness around, something could happen — she could pass by us. I waved off this intuition in the name of bullshit, but 30 seconds later I was proved heavily wrong as I stared in amazement, with you looking downcast and walking past.

February 13th  ·  9:10 PM to 10:40 PM

The Epic Pursuit

I will be honest about this evening: it was madness, and I knew it was madness even as I was inside it, and this knowledge did nothing whatsoever to slow me down. This is perhaps the truest thing I can say about the particular variety of devotion I have been carrying — it does not respond to reason. It does not negotiate with the rational mind.

The evening began near MT hall, where I had been standing with two of my best mates on the raised platform near Tikka, outside The Yellow Straw, after a dinner of wraps. Through the swirling, chaotic dim light that encompasses the MT cycling track, from a distance of more than sixty meters, I found you. This was not eyesight. From around 80 meters, under the particular quality of evening campus lighting, the identification was made by something older and less explicable — the navigational sense that certain creatures develop for certain others, the way a compass needle, regardless of what is placed between it and the pole, always resolves to north. You were with your trio; me with mine. We became shadows trailing a light source, maintaining a ghostly distance through the evening streets. We were initially planning to let go and move towards our normal path, but these two guys I have the absolute fortune of knowing believe it is their utmost destiny to pull me towards you.

Up to the Hijli top, and then down again, until your group arrived at Amritulya. I stationed myself outside and performed the pantomime of a person casually existing, while every watt of my attention was tunnelled through the ordering counter window like a beam through a lens.

You ordered Tulsi tea. I need to sit with this for a moment, because it is too perfect to move past quickly. Of all the things you could have ordered — the cold drinks, the coffees, the catalogue of hot and cold and sweet and salty — you chose Tulsi tea. Earthy. Grounding. Slightly bitter. Herbal in a way that is old and medicinal and carries in it the memory of many generations of quiet wisdom. The absolute perfect beverage for a woman who studies the deepest histories of the earth and carries in her face the accumulated gravity of feeling she doesn't advertise. Of course you ordered Tulsi tea. The universe, in this as in so many things, is far more literary than we give it credit for.

I retreated toward the railway tracks under the pretext of drinking tea, and when you finally emerged from the shop you were a vision that made the surrounding darkness seem not dark at all but full — full of the specific quality of your presence, the way a dark room is full of silence, which is not an absence but a texture. I watched you for three or four minutes that felt like the pages of a long and beloved novel — the kind you read slowly because you know the ending is coming and cannot bear to reach it. By 10:40 PM, with hope disappearing, I did too — en route I saw your friend with your other friends talking on that SNVH-MT bench, so I knew you were back to your room.

February 20th  ·  8:40 PM

The Radiant Anomaly

Every artist who has ever attempted to paint light will tell you that the most technically demanding light to capture is not moonlight or firelight or the golden hour before sunset. It is, counterintuitively, joy. Joy produces a quality of light that has no stable form — it does not come from outside a person but from within, moving through the skin and the eyes and the particular configuration of a smiling face in a way that the usual optical rules cannot account for.

I saw you that evening at the Pepsi cut — casual blue t-shirt, shorts, your easy and unannounced presence in the world — and you were incandescent. Not with the usual quiet luminescence I had catalogued over the preceding months. Something different was happening. You were smiling — a full, genuine, uninhibited, toothy smile that engaged your whole face, that reached all the way to the places your smiles usually stopped short of, that carried in it not the ghost of melancholy but the pure, clean, unburdened frequency of happiness arriving and being entirely, unambiguously welcomed. To see you so thoroughly devoid of that trademark gravity was like watching a beloved landscape after a brief thunderstorm that passes quickly and leaves the air washed clean. It warmed me in ways that no external source ever has. I went home that evening carrying your smile in my chest like a small, cupped flame, sheltered from the wind, walking carefully so as not to extinguish it.

The Geographical Echo

There is a concept in music called a reprise — the return of an earlier theme, transformed by everything that has come between its first statement and its reappearance. The same notes, the same progression, but now weighted with memory, with the accumulated meaning of all the development that preceded it. March 10th was a reprise, and the effect was precisely as described above: more resonant, more complex, richer for the interval.

I found you standing in the exact same spot where January 20th had delivered its epiphany — the same coordinates, the same agricultural gate footpath, as though you had been standing there continuously for fifty days and the world had simply rotated around you. You wore a pristine white t-shirt that caught the March sunlight differently from the way the January maroon sweatshirt had caught the winter light — this was brighter, more open, a colour that gives rather than absorbs — and those familiar light blue jeans, and the Crocs. The consistency of you. Your particular, unwavering aesthetic self-possession in the face of whatever the world's changing conditions might suggest. There is a philosophy in this consistency — the philosophy of a person who knows who they are and has no interest in renegotiating the terms.

To stand in the same place where a revelation once occurred and find the source of that revelation standing in the same place is to encounter, briefly, the sensation that time is not linear but recursive — that the best moments of one's life return, not identically but essentially, carrying their original charge. The quality of feeling your presence produced in me was indistinguishable from what I had felt on January 20th. Perhaps slightly more certain. Perhaps slightly more inhabited. The way a handwriting becomes more distinctly one's own the more one practices it.

The Front Row Scholar

There is something almost unbearably intimate about encountering a person you admire inside the architecture of their ordinary life — not in the broad campus thoroughfares where the encounter is brief and public and over before it has properly begun, but in the contained, quiet interior of a shared space where the encounter has duration. NC312. The late morning light fell across your shoulder from the classroom window, and in it I could see the particular concentration that your face achieves when you are genuinely engaged: a slight softening of the brow, a stillness of the features that speaks of an interior process quietly proceeding. And to think, I reached the Hydrogen Technology class 10 minutes late to see you — it wasn't a loss.

A Slapstick Tragedy

I want to be honest about this evening: it was, by any objective measure, an embarrassment. And I want to include it here, in this ledger that I have tried to keep honest, because the truth of what I am documenting is not only composed of the beautiful moments — the luminous sightings, the quiet epiphanies, the six-minute eternities and the evenings of Tulsi tea. March 17th  ·  10:04 AM

The Terrifying Spark

I have been trying, for some weeks now, to find language adequate to what happened outside the TP class on this Tuesday morning in March, and I am beginning to suspect that the experience itself is resistant to language — that it belongs to the category of things that can be circled and approached from many angles without ever being precisely named. Let me try anyway, because trying is all I have.

You were dressed that morning in a short, bluish-dotted kurti paired with black lowers and your faithful Crocs — an outfit that had a lightness to it, a spring-adjacency, as though your wardrobe had registered the calendar's progression before the weather had fully committed. And then it happened. Our eyes met. This alone was not unusual — our eyes had met before, in August, in the preliminary encounter that inaugurated everything. But what happened on March 17th was different in its quality, different in its texture and its weight. Because in this meeting of eyes I felt, with a certainty I have never felt about anything before or since, that something registered. Not recognition in the full sense. But recognition in a narrower, more terrifying sense: your eyes located me. They did not slide past me the way eyes slide past furniture or background architecture. They stopped. For a fraction of a second that lasted much longer than its official duration, you looked at me in the particular way that one looks at a thing one has seen before but cannot quite place — the slight narrowing, the almost-question, the beginning of a file being opened in the mind's archive.

I felt a jolt down my spine so specific and complete that I cannot describe it as anything other than electrical. The invisible string I had been holding for months pulled suddenly, violently taut, and for a fraction of a second we were connected by it — not romantically, not transactionally, but in the way two nodes in a circuit are connected: a current passed, briefly and without announcement, between us. You looked away. I stopped breathing until you did. And then you were gone around the corner, and I was standing outside the TP class with the residual current still humming in my chest, aware that something had irrevocably changed in the cartography of this thing between us, even if the new cartography was not, and would never be, a map with two people on it.

The Archaeology of Memory

Near the foyer of NC312, between the first and second floor landing — a liminal space, threshold architecture, the kind of place where encounters happen that are too brief to be events but too weighted to be forgotten. You were ascending with your friend, both of you engaged with your phones in the particular posture of modern transit: the downward gaze, the occasional look-up to verify that the physical world has not rearranged itself inconveniently. You wore loose blue jeans, Crocs, and a top — and the top is the reason I am writing this entry with such care, because the top was not merely a garment on this occasion. It was a temporal event.

Perhaps that is the final indignity of admiration: it can render a man proficient in the abstruse intricacies of algorithms and statistical learning while leaving him utterly inept at the deceptively simple task of saying hello. Yet I could not persuade myself to perform the simplest inference of all: that a conversation need not be a confession, that an introduction need not be a declaration of intent, and that getting to know another human being need not begin with the expectation that they might become the love of one's life.

You may remain an unanswered question in the margins of my college years, a faint, golden annotation in an otherwise demanding chapter of my life. But I must continue writing the chapter. And for once, I think I can do so without waiting to see whether you appear on the next page.

And that, folks, is a story for another day.

These are parallel lines. They do not intersect. They endure.

This ledger was written for

Simran Jangral

of Samba, Jammu & Kashmir

She came from a small town called Samba, tucked into the gentle hills of Jammu — a place where the winters are honest and the light on stone walls carries the particular quality of a place that does not need to prove itself to anyone. She carried that quality with her. She always did.

I do not know if she will ever read this. I do not know if she would recognise herself in these pages — whether the woman documented here, with her luminescent melancholy and her geological depth and her breath-of-fresh-air presence, bears sufficient resemblance to the woman she encounters every morning in her own mirror. I suspect not, entirely. The subject of devotion is always, in some final sense, a collaboration between the beloved and the beholder. What is written here is my half of that collaboration: the only half I was ever permitted.

What I know with certainty is this: she was, for the better part of two years, the most beautiful thing in my world. She was the reason that Tuesday afternoons contained something worth noticing. She was the ground bass beneath the noise of every ordinary day. She was, each time — each single, unrepeatable, unearned time — a breath of fresh air.

This ledger is hers. Every word of it has always been hers. It is offered now with no expectation of return, no hope of acknowledgment, and all the love that parallel lines are capable of — which is, it turns out, a very great deal.
